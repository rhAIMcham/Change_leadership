• 2003 - 2005

    Early days

    Skype's early days, 2003–2005
  • August 29, 2003

    Skype is launched

    The early 2000s was a period of rapid globalisation and technological development, facilitated by increased accessibility to the Internet and online networks. However, making long-distance phone calls was still an expensive and complicated process. Recognising the need for faster, cheaper communication, a small team of developers created Skype, and pioneered consumer voice and video calling around the world.

  • December, 2004

    1 million users

    The impact was immediate. On the first day of public release, over 100, 000 people had tried Skype. By the end of their first year, Skype had over a million simultaneous online users at peak. The small size of the company allowed for rapid iteration and improvement, and the free-to-use model rapidly expanded its userbase. By the end of 2005, just two years after they had launched, Skype had 54 million registered users. Skype was built using peer-to peer technology that kept start-up costs low and reliable while supporting high-quality calls, garnering public trust and positive brand association.

  • 2005 - 2009

    Tenure at eBay

    Skype under eBay ownership, 2005–2009
  • September, 2005

    Acquired by eBay

    Skype’s time as a private company was brief. After rocketing to popularity in Europe, Skype caught the attention of another early internet titan, eBay, and was acquired by eBay for $2.6 billion in 2005. Previously, Skype had primarily been used for personal calls or by smaller business, but eBay saw a different future for Skype. As then CEO Meg Whitman told The New York Times, the plan was to integrate Skype into eBay’s online marketplace, allowing vendors and purchasers to communicate more effectively. They also hoped this would enable Skype to grow in the business world, “particularly with transactions involving real estate, big-ticket purchases, and services that require detailed conversations.”

    Why did eBay want Skype?

    Beyond the headline price tag, the acquisition reflected eBay’s belief that voice communication could reduce friction in online transactions – buyers and sellers negotiating big-ticket items might be more amicable over a call rather than back-and-forth messages.

  • December, 2005

    Video calling arrives

    Skype cemented their place as an early Internet juggernaut by introducing video calling in 2005. For the first time, it was possible to make international calls through video feed, connecting people all around the world. This was so successful that Skype’s user count hit 54 million people just before the company’s second birthday. Crucially, the capacity to video-call also influenced modern business practice. Communication with another office or team far away was no longer impersonal, as colleagues could call each other so long as they both had an Internet connection.

  • 2007 – 2009

    Video revolution

    The video calling revolution, 2007–2009
  • 2007

    Tensions between eBay and Skype

    During a period of increasing tension, the Estonian founders left the company after several clashes with eBay’s American corporate culture. Despite the technical team introducing file sharing and calling using existing phone numbers to the platform, eBay proved to be a hostile environment for Skype. Wanting to cement their market position, new CEO Josh Silverman encouraged the development of Skype 4.0 in mid 2009. This release prioritised video functionality and pushed Skype to market dominance in the US.

  • 2009

    Video in focus

    This focus on video calls marked a significant change, as Skype had previously focused on voice calls, with video as a supplemental feature. This launch preceded facetime on the iPhone 4, which would arrive the next June, and positioned Skype as a significant influence on communication and culture, growing their userbase to 500 million global users and cementing their presence in the United States.

  • September 2009

    Departing eBay

    Despite this success, internal tensions between eBay and Skype continued to escalate, and newly appointed CEO John Donahoe wanted to end the conflict. While initially proposing that Skype begin transitioning to an IPO, in September 2009 eBay announced that they would sell a 65% stake in Skype to an investment group. This group was comprised of Silver Lake Partners, Index Ventures, the Canadian Pension Plan Investment Board, and Andreesen Horowitz, with a total valuation at $2.7 billion. By this point, Skype was gaining about 380, 000 new users each day and generating $740 million in annual revenue, making the company a very appealing acquisition.

  • 2010

    660 million registered users

    Skype under new VC leadership, 2010
  • 2010

    VC transformation

    2010 was another key turning point for Skype. The VC controlling stake brought in former Cisco FM Tony Bates, and a product lead in Sandhya Venkatachalam to build a more robust and self-sufficient company. Improvements in management structuring within Skype set the company on the path to an IPO, but this would not last.

  • 2011 - 2015

    Beginnings at Microsoft

    Skype's early years at Microsoft, 2011–2015
  • 2011

    Early adoptions

    In 2011, Microsoft agreed to acquire Skype for $8.5 billion. At the time, this was the most expensive purchase in Microsoft’s history. The company had big plans for Skype – it would be crucial to centralising their varied communication pathways into one key tool. Calls, messages and video chats would all run through Skype. Skype software would appear in Windows Office, Xbox and the now defunct Microsoft Phone.

  • 2013

    Technical challenges

    By 2013, Microsoft would be forced to undertake their first major challenge with Skype: upgrading the technical backend software. This involved moving Skype from its original peer-to-peer (P2P) technology to the modern (and still widely used) Cloud computing technology emerging at the time. While P2P had been innovative in 2003, it was difficult to update and maintain. However, changing the core processes of the platform exposed users to ongoing performance and reliability issues, with lagging between mobile and desktop versions of the software being common. Additionally, some older devices were incompatible with the new version of Skype, forcing users to update their personal computing systems to stay connected.

    Despite being acquired by a tech giant, Microsoft had opted to keep many of Skype’s key features from the VC rebuild. While this allowed Skype to retain its identity as a company, it also meant the full resources of the Microsoft technical empire were not available to Skype. For consumers, this meant what should be a seamless experience provided by one of the largest tech companies in the world felt buggy and incomplete.

  • 2014

    Split in two

    Skype was split into two products – Skype for business and Skype for personal use. Microsoft had an existing business calls tool Microsoft Lync, which integrated Skype’s technology and was rebranded to Skype for business. While this is a common practice for Microsoft products – such as Microsoft Word for home or Word for Office – this pushed Skype for the consumer into a new space that required redefinition. Skype for business was also distributed to the office team internally, further diluting the brand and creating experiential differences between the two products.

  • 2015

    Teams on the horizon

    During this period in 2015, Saleforce’s new tool Slack was becoming more popular in the business world, as it could scale easily for enterprise. Internal reports from Microsoft show their product teams were under pressure to produce a rival product, and rather than using this moment to focus all their efforts on a rebrand for Skype, now fully transitioned to the cloud, they instead planned a new product: Skype Teams.

  • 2016 – 2019

    Slow decline

    Skype's slow decline, 2016–2019
  • 2016

    Struggling in a diverse industry

    By the time the product was officially announced in 2016, it had been renamed to Microsoft Teams. While still utilising some of Skype’s technology, Skype was invisible in the released product and Skype for business had become redundant overnight. Skype consumer was left in an odd position – after its removal from integration within Outlook and Word, Skype was left without a clear direction.

    This enterprise focus on Teams also increasingly reduced engineering focus on Skype, and the product lagged in the mobile era. As users experienced issues with calls, inbuilt microphones, and the notification functionality, many defected to the growing number of other options. Teams was now available at work, Slack had introduced video calling in early 2016, Whatsapp began video calling by 2016, and Zoom launched in 2013. Skype’s dominance was slipping, and it was rapidly losing its relevance in the industry.

  • 2017

    Rebranding

    In 2017, Skype launched its answer to this challenge, and it proved wildly unpopular. The redesign was an attempt to push Skype into the present moment, with a mobile-first interface designed to reflect prominent social media platforms. It included features like ‘Highlights,’ which users were quick to identify were very similar to Snapchat’s stories functionality. Accessing key functions like chat and call had been complicated, which alienated users. Ratings plummeted in the Google and Apple app stores, and online criticism of the redesign was very clear. The backlash was so great that a year later, Microsoft released a new update removing some of the more controversial features, and returning to a more familiar layout.

  • 2018 - 2019

    Rebranding

    Over the next two years, Skype slowly lost market share to Teams, Slack and Zoom, but the final moment would arrive with the Covid-19 pandemic.

  • 2020 - 2021

    Working from home during the lockdowns

    Lockdown-era video calling, 2020–2021
  • 2020 - 2021

    Sudden surge

    For the first time in years, many users logged into Zoom and brought their workplace into their homes to connect with their team members across the world. Skype was wholly unprepared for this, losing millions of users to Zoom over a period of several months. Zoom’s daily user count in December 2019 was 10 million – by April 2020 it was more than 300 million. Skype enjoyed an early bump in numbers, raising 70% to about 40 million daily users in March before steadily declining. Due to the sudden increase in demand for video-call software, Skype’s technical weaknesses and poor integration into the Microsoft environment were undeniable.

    Skype could not recover from this position, having failed to move laterally into the social media space and now clearly outclassed by competitors in the communication space. Microsoft concentrated their resources on improving Teams, and for the first release of Windows 11 in October 2021, Teams replaced Zoom as the default communications app.

  • 2022 - present

    Final days

    Skype's final days, 2022–present
  • 2022 - present day

    Final goodbyes

    Left without updates or significant support, Skype slowly lost users and visibility over the next four years, with Microsoft announcing its formal closure in 2025, closing the book on one of the web’s early success stories.
