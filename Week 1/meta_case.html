• Changing the narrative

    Meta’s transition from the Facebook brand toward the metaverse is an example of how ambitious organisational change becomes a challenge when the proposed future does not align with customer needs or market behaviour. In October 2021, Facebook officially changed its corporate name to Meta and positioned the metaverse as the company’s new technological focus. CEO Mark Zuckerberg described the metaverse as a more immersive form of Internet use that could unite socialising, work, entertainment, education and commerce into one platform. Rather than launch another product, Meta’s strategy was to transform the company’s identity and position it as the innovator of a new generation of computing.

  • What is a 'metaverse?'

    The metaverse is a term referring to a shared online virtual world where users create customisable avatars and interact with 3D environments such as cities or shops. The Meta specific virtual reality platform was an $80 billion video game called Horizon Worlds, and at the peak of its hype there were projections that as many as 2.6 billion users would be online by 2030.

    The video alongside is a promotional video from Meta, released in 2022. It shows an artistic rendition of how Meta leadership imagined the metaverse might look. It is important to note that the real user experience was not this seamless - legs did not arrive for user avatars until September 2023, almost a year after they were first shown to users.

  • Reception to becoming 'Meta'

    This change was met with significant criticism. The Facebook brand had been embroiled in several high-profile scandals, such as the Cambridge Analytica leaks and other ongoing privacy concerns culminating in a $5 billion fine from the FTC in 2019, so many consumers viewed this rebrand as an attempt for Meta to distance itself from controversy. Customers were reluctant to share additional personal data with an organisation known for hacking and leaks, and although initial estimates of user engagement were high, others did not feel Meta would be capable of digitally replicating actual human interaction in an enjoyable way. Meta also had not made any significant technological developments in the virtual reality (VR) space, which already had an activity community of users engaged on other platforms, and thus failed to attract their own userbase. While there was significant corporate and celebrity interest in Horizon Worlds, without the initial popular initial appeal of Facebook, the metaverse would not be viable long term.

  • Challenges adapting internally

    The change also created challenges within the organisation, as using the metaverse was incorporated as a work tool for meetings and other communication despite its inefficiency and frequent stalling during use. The company’s 2021 strategy involved significant long-term investment into their future platform, but there was a gap between the ambition of the strategy and the practical experience of using the product itself. Limited adoption and unclear use cases can reinforce each other; fewer users can mean fewer reasons for creators and businesses to invest, while limited content and participation can make the product less attractive for potential users. Combined with marketplace redundancy, despite being positioned as a revolutionary technology, Horizon Worlds and Meta’s metaverse was left without a relevant spot in the VR space, and in March 2026 Meta announced that while existing games would continue to be supported, no new products would be added to the platform.

  • Lessons learnt

    From a change-leadership perspective, Meta shows the importance of testing assumptions and continuously responding to evidence. The organisation identified a technological trend and committed to becoming an early leader, but the experience demonstrates that technological innovation alone does not guarantee successful organisational change. Leaders must also understand what customers actually find important, distinguish an emerging trend from a sustainable market need, and be prepared to adjust their strategy when data challenges their original assumptions.
