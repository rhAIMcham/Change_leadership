• Getting started

    In 1991 husband and wife Sandy and Julie Cameron established their first farm together in Meredith, Victoria. Both from farming families, they weren’t strangers to the challenges and demands of farm life. Unfortunately, they embarked on this journey at a difficult time; Australia was in a severe drought for much of the 1990s, and the price of wool dropped sharply, rendering their initial plan untenable. Exploring other options, they tried their hand at dairy farming at the suggestion of a family friend.


    The established dairy industry within Australia is focused on cattle and often operates at massive scale. The Camerons wanted to do things a little bit differently and opted to use their existing sheep flock to explore more exciting and unconventional dairy farming. This was no easy feat – their first attempt yielded only 200mls of milk. However, using Sandy’s expertise as a vet, they gradually improved the quality of their flock, increasing milk production and milk quality. Their sheep graze in fields, giving them access to wild grasses and plants, improving their health, quality of life and the taste and quantity of their milk supply.

  • Finding their rhythm

    As milk production from their sheep stabilised, they experimented with different products. Yogurt, blue cheese and other dairy items didn’t see the commercial success they had been hoping for, but over time they developed their now famous marinated sheep and goat cheese. They now have 3500 sheep and 12,000 goats on their farm, making them the largest sheep dairy farm in Australia.


    Once they established their what (sheep, goats, high-quality specialty cheeses) they turned their attention to the how. Farming can be very hard on the natural environment, but working on a small Australian farm, maintaining and improving the quality of their land became an important part of making the farm profitable. They use manure from their flocks to fertilise their land rather than relying on commercial fertilisers, use grey water to irrigate trees and have installed 100 kilowatts of solar panels on the roofs of their dairy sheds. Additionally, in 2015 they invested in a wood biomass boiler, which meets around 70% of the site’s heat requirements. Dairy farming is an energetically intense process, so making these changes when they can allows them to reduce their environmental impact and preserve the quality of the grazing areas for the sheep and goats.


  • Solidifying their brand

    “Taking care of the environment is central to what makes Meredith Dairy what it is” said Julie Cameron. “Our Vision is to produce food sustainably and we’re really serious about that. We want to ensure that everything we do considers the environment, and we want to inspire others to do the same.”


    The Camerons have dedicated time, money and focus to improving their farming practices for the benefit of the consumer, the animals and their land. This has not been without its challenges – in 2019, not long after their winning of the Royal Agricultural Society of NSW President's Medal in 2019, cameras leaked footage of their goats being disbudded, a practice where a hot iron is briefly applied to the horn bud. While standard practice within the dairy industry and thus unremarkable to Sandy and Julie who grew up around farms, the controversial footage raised questions from the public around how compatible the wholesome, environmentally and ethically responsible image Meredith projects is with the behind-the-scenes reality of farming life. As disconnection between food and animal grows in the public consciousness, these issues and challenges become more common and difficult to practically address. Sandy Cameron said when interviewed that the goats were given meloxicam, a slow-release pain medicine prior to the disbudding procedure.

  • Ongoing identity

    In response to the exposure, Sandy Cameron committed to ensuring anaesthetic would always be used on such procedures and remarked “The dream is to breed a polled goat, but that lies in the future”. As with crops grown to be more resistant to drought and pests, animals can also be bred to be more resilient, productive, or in this case safer for workers and other animals. In an ever-changing environment, using old and practiced methods while continually looking to improve and innovate gives Meredith dairy and edge over their competition.


    This investment has paid dividends. In 2014, they won for their Dill Chèvre goats cheese at Nantwich International Cheese Awards, and in 2019 they won for their Marinated Goat Cheese, original goats cheese and mixed milk jar (Marinated sheep and goat blend). They have won across many categories in Australian cheese awards, and are a fridge staple across the country.
