• 2007

    Identifying a need

    Caption: Identifying a need, 2007
  • 2007

    A problem worth solving

    In 2007, founder and future CEO Melanie Perkins was a student at the University of Western Australia studying communications, psychology and commerce. When assisting her classmates with different design software, she experienced how difficult and complex these programs were. Even creating simple designs required significant skill as the tools were intended for professionals or experienced hobbyists, not the casual user trying to solve a simple problem.
    This sparked an idea – simplifying design tools and serving the average consumer directly. Rapid technological innovation, such as Facebook’s launch in 2004 and the release of the first iPhone in 2007, integrated the Internet as an accessible resource. Perkins identified that a web-based tool with a free offering would be popular.

  • Fusion Books

    The first iteration

    The first iteration of this idea was Fusion Books. This tool was designed to create yearbooks, targeted at high school and university students. The interface was purposely simple, with users uploading images that were placed in pre-built templates.
    The success of this venture convinced Perkins and co-founder Cliff Obrecht that something with a much broader scope was possible.

  • 2010

    Fusion Books to Canva

    Caption: Fusion Books to Canva, 2010
  • 2010

    Searching for investment

    Perkins and Obrecht brainstormed ideas for their new online design tool and began looking for potential investors. This kind of tool would require highly skilled engineers to build and maintain, requiring a significant capital investment to get started.

  • 2010

    A chance meeting

    Funding did not arrive quickly. After being rejected numerous times, early Zoom backer Bill Tai encountered Perkins and Obrecht by chance at a networking event in Perth and chose to support the project. Using his connections to other potential investors in Silicon Valley, they began recruiting engineering talent to build out their new platform, Canva.

  • 2013 – 2019

    Massive growth

    Caption: Massive growth, 2013–2019
  • 2013

    Launch

    Canva’s first 6 years were incredibly successful. They launched in August 2013 to a waitlist of 50,000 Australian users, and by early 2015 they had over 100,000 users. The intuitive interface and comprehensive suite of templates, icons, stock images and other resources made personalised digital media successful and intuitive. With its free tier, Canva also gave users access to experiment with the tool before signing up for the subscription service.

  • 2015

    Canva for Work

    In 2015, they launched Canva for Work (now Canva Pro). This paid tier was a change in delivery model – the platform had originally operated with the main functions free, while premium photos, fonts and other assets behind a small paywall. Introducing this subscription service granted access to higher quality access for a fixed price. Canva experienced rapid success, with its user base growing to 3.6 million by the end of 2016.

  • 2017

    First profit

    By 2017, Canva recorded its first profit, reaching the milestone of 10 million users and 1 billion USD valuation the following year. As the capability of the Canva platform expanded, different tools were integrated such as animation and presentation functionalities, increasing Canva’s appeal to larger businesses and organisations.

  • 2019

    Enterprise offering

    In 2019, Canva capitalised on this growth by launching an enterprise offering, allowing the integration of Canva into workflows for businesses, rising their monthly users to 24 million.

  • 2020

    The pandemic and new user heights

    Caption: The pandemic and new user heights, 2020
  • 2020

    Pandemic surge

    In 2020, the pandemic facilitated the popularisation of digital presentation and online communication. Canva usage soared as workplaces transitioned to a digital, online format. This new user base pushed Canva’s valuation to 6 billion USD.

  • 2020 – 2021

    Video editing

    Canva also added video editing capability onto its platform in 2020. With TikTok and shortform video sites growing in popularity, Canva’s export pipeline and prebuilt templates made it a very attractive tool for posting on social media. This positive growth continued, with 2021 seeing 75 million monthly users and a 40B USD valuation.

  • 2022

    Canva for Teams

    Caption: Canva for Teams, 2022
  • 2022

    Teams and the Visual Worksuite

    Canva for Teams was launched in 2022 to capitalise on the new enterprise user base. In late 2022, Canva also expanded to the Canva Visual Worksuite, offering different design creation tools for documents, presentations, social media posts and videos.

  • 2022

    Market turbulence

    When the broader tech industry saw valuation and tumbling stock prices, Canva was not immune to this change. Their valuation dipped significantly, but by the end of 2022 had largely recovered.

  • 2023 – 2024

    Introduction of AI

    Caption: Introduction of AI, 2023–2024
  • 2023

    First AI tools

    By 2023, Canva hit 135 million monthly users, and launched their first AI tools to their existing suite of products.
    With Text to Image and copywriting assistance, Canva tested the appetite of its userbase for integrated AI tools. These tools were powered by OpenAI, but the positive reception to their inclusion stoked appetite within Canva to expand their AI capabilities.

  • 2024

    Price rises and AI investment

    In 2024, Canva significantly increased subscription prices, with some individual users and smaller businesses facing increases of up to 300%. The additional revenue supported major investment in AI, including the acquisition of Sydney-based AI generation tool Leonardo and the development of Canva 2.0, a ground-up rebuild designed to make text-driven AI generation central to the platform. However, these changes were initially internal, with AI tools remaining optional rather than becoming the focus of the everyday user experience.

  • 2026

    Canva 2.0 and the cost of AI

    Caption: Canva 2.0 and the cost of AI, 2026
  • April 2026

    Canva 2.0 launches

    Canva launched Canva 2.0, putting AI generation at the centre of its platform. This received generally positive user feedback as its monthly user base reached 265 million. This updated version also streamlined integration across workflows, allowing users to port information and styling from other tools directly into Canva AI, creating a more seamless experience. However, this increased functionality introduced a significant obstacle: AI inference is expensive, and Canva’s reliance on external frontier models while its own models were still developing meant that even limited AI use by free users was generating substantial and unexpected costs.

    What is AI inference?

    AI inference is the process of an AI model using what it has learned to generate a new output, such as text, images or other data, in response to a user’s request. Each inference requires computing power and resources, meaning every AI interaction can create a cost for the organisation providing the service.

  • 2026

    Resetting the growth target

    Canva’s growing AI expenses challenged its traditional free-to-paid business model, prompting CEO Melanie Perkins to reduce the 2026 revenue growth target from 30% to 20% while slowing the AI rollout to rebuild infrastructure, reduce costs and create a more sustainable business model.

  • 2026

    Investor and industry reaction

    This had an immediate impact on their valuations; key American investment firms T. Rowe Price and Franklin Templeton dropped their valuations of Canva by 9 and 6 percent respectively since the start of 2026. These firms manage 1.9 and 1.8 trillion USD between them, and their valuation of Canva at 42 billion USD had been crucial in establishing its peak valuation.

    Although Canva is privately owned, lowering its growth forecast could still affect investor confidence and the funding needed for its costly AI transition. Similar forecast reductions from Figma and challenges facing Adobe highlighted broader pressures across the design industry, while Canva responded by accelerating investment in its internal AI capabilities, expanding its team to more than 200 staff to reduce reliance on costly external models and make AI generation more financially sustainable.

  • Several months later

    Cost reductions

    Several months later, Canva had transformed this investment into results. Internal models were now more efficient, and the cheapest possible models were being successfully assigned to simple problems so complex tasks could be managed by the more expensive and powerful AI tools.

    Overview of Canva’s cost reductions across their AI functionality:

    MeasureResult
    AI-task cost reduction~90% over ~3 months
    Image model cost vs. frontier~30x cheaper
    Video model cost vs. frontier~17x cheaper
    Style-transfer model cost vs. frontier~23x cheaper
  • Present

    Towards Canva 2.1

    Canva is preparing to launch Canva 2.1 with the AI functionality users value at a more sustainable cost. By developing their own AI tools internally, Canva ensured their content aligned with Canva’s brand and values. While this is a positive outcome, AI disruption remains an issue for other design platforms, and stock fall for Canva’s publicly listed peers Figma and Adobe indicates that this is a broader trend that will continue.
    However, Canva’s decision to slow down, address its financial and technical challenges, and invest in its own AI capabilities has reportedly reduced AI production costs by 90 percent, strengthening the platform’s ability to scale AI sustainably into the future.
